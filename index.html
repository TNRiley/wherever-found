Wherever Found

Every species the Endangered Species Act has ever listed, delisted or reclassified — pulled from the two agencies' own live records, crossed against four years of the spending reports Congress is sent, and the number of whales it is legal to kill.

The Endangered Species Act became law on December 28, 1973, the culmination of a decade of growing alarm about species disappearing faster than anyone could count them. It does one specific thing: it lets two federal agencies — the Fish and Wildlife Service for everything on land and in fresh water, NOAA Fisheries for everything in the ocean and migrating through it — add a species, subspecies or distinct population to a legal list, after which harming it, trading it or destroying the habitat it depends on becomes a federal offense.

This page is that list, kept current against the government's own database rather than a news summary of it. It is not built to alarm. Most of what follows is procedural: paperwork, dates, review cycles, and the occasional very large number. The interesting part is what the paperwork itself reveals once you can see all of it at once — including that "removed from the list" is not the same thing as "recovered," that the most-listed group of organisms in the country is not an animal at all, and that one species has been legally endangered, threatened, experimental and delisted-as-recovered at the same time, in different states.

Since 1973

A list that grows in bursts, not a line

Every bar is a calendar year; its height is how many of today's 2,619 tracked listing entities — entries in the Fish and Wildlife Service's Endangered Species database, current and historical — were first listed that year, whether or not they are still on the list today. The tallest years are not evenly spaced: a single day, June 2, 1970, added 248 species at once — almost entirely foreign mammals, birds and reptiles listed under the Act's 1969 predecessor law — and another single day, June 14, 1976, added 147 more. Those two dates alone outweigh most individual decades of one-at-a-time review that followed.

Two words, four categories

What "listed" actually means

The law recognizes two core categories — endangered (in danger of extinction now) and threatened (likely to become endangered) — plus two narrower legal constructions: an experimental population released into new territory under relaxed rules to encourage recovery attempts, and a similarity-of-appearance listing, for a species that isn't actually at risk but is protected because it's easily mistaken for one that is.

The flagship finding

"Delisted" does not mean "recovered"

A species leaves the list for one of three reasons, and the government's own bookkeeping treats all three as the same event: delisted. Only one of those reasons is the good news the word implies.

Of the — delisting entities on record, — were removed because they actually recovered — the bald eagle (2007), the Louisiana black bear (2016), the Northern Rocky Mountain gray wolf (2017) among them. — were removed because they went extinct first. The rest were never really eligible in the first place: a taxonomic reclassification decided the "species" was actually a hybrid or a duplicate of something else already covered, or new information overturned the original listing decision. Reading a falling delisting count as an environmental success story, without checking which bucket each one fell into, is the trap this page is built to avoid.

Up and down

Reclassification: the list can move both ways

A species can also be reclassified without leaving the list at all — downlisted from endangered to the less severe threatened as it recovers, or uplisted the other way as its situation worsens. Below are the — reclassification events this build could match to a specific listing entity and date.

Two agencies, one law

Land, water, and the species that cross the line

The Fish and Wildlife Service leads on almost everything — mammals, birds, plants, insects, freshwater fish. NOAA Fisheries takes over the moment a species' life happens mostly in salt water: whales, most sea turtles, Pacific salmon runs, reef-building corals, the big migratory sharks. A small number are split between both agencies at once, usually because the species crosses that line itself — a sea turtle that nests on a beach and swims across an ocean.

Section 18 · the money

Seven percent of the list, two-thirds of the money

Section 18 of the Act obliges the Fish and Wildlife Service to tell Congress, every year, what every federal agency and every state actually spent on every listed species. The reports are published as PDFs with no machine-readable release; the four most recent — fiscal years 2019 through 2022, the last of them published in May 2026 — were parsed for this page and reconcile to the dollar against the totals the reports print themselves.

They show something the listing counts hide completely. NOAA Fisheries leads — of the listing entities on the whole list. It accounts for — of the money.

NOAA Fisheries

The other agency's own list

Everything above this point comes from the Fish and Wildlife Service's database, including its opinion of which species NOAA Fisheries leads. NOAA keeps its own list, and publishes it as JSON. It contains — species broken into — separately listed entities, and it carries three things FWS's export has no column for at all: which NOAA region leads the species, whether a recovery plan exists yet, and whether critical habitat was designated, refused, or never attempted.

Recovery plans

The Act requires a recovery plan for every listed species unless one would not promote conservation. These are NOAA's own status labels for its own entities.

Critical habitat

“Not prudent” is a real finding an agency can make — usually where publishing a map would tell collectors exactly where to go.

Two agencies, two taxonomies

Joining NOAA's list to FWS's turns out to be a data problem rather than a lookup. The two agencies do not use the same scientific names for the same animals, and neither one flags the difference.

Globally secure, locally endangered

NatureServe ranks a species across its entire global range, from G1 (critically imperilled) to G5 (demonstrably secure). Running every NOAA-managed species through that scale exposes what the ESA is actually for: it does not list species, it lists populations. A salmon species can be abundant across the Pacific Rim and functionally extinct in one river system, and the second fact is the one the Act responds to.

Potential Biological Removal

The number of animals it is legal to kill

For marine mammals, a second law runs alongside the ESA and produces a number the ESA never does. The Marine Mammal Protection Act makes NOAA publish, for every stock in US waters, a Potential Biological Removal level: how many animals a year people can kill — nets, ship strikes, gunshots, entanglement — while still letting the stock reach and hold its optimum size. Alongside it the same report prints the observed human-caused deaths. Two numbers, same page, directly comparable.

Of the — Atlantic and Gulf stocks where NOAA publishes both figures, — are being killed faster than NOAA itself says is sustainable.

The paperwork

What the Act looks like from the Federal Register

Every listing, delisting, critical habitat designation, permit and finding has to be published in the Federal Register, which has a proper documented API. Asking it for everything either agency has filed under “Endangered and Threatened” gives a second, independent view of the same law — and the two agencies turn out to do visibly different jobs under it.

Checking back in

The five-year review

The Act requires FWS and NOAA Fisheries to formally revisit every listed species at least once every five years and ask whether its status still fits the evidence. — completed reviews are on record. The honest headline: — of them concluded "no change in status" — the review process mostly confirms what was already known, not a steady drumbeat of upgrades and downgrades.

Methods & limits

What this page can and can't tell you

Every number on this page comes from live queries against the Fish and Wildlife Service's ECOSphere reporting API (ecos.fws.gov), fetched fresh at build time — not a static export someone else cleaned first. The endpoints are undocumented; they were found by watching the network traffic of FWS's own public report pages and are as likely to break as any unversioned government API. The fetch and merge scripts are in this project's src/ folder.

A few specific traps, found the hard way while building this:

  • "Listed species" and "listing entities" are not the same count. A single species can be listed more than once under different Distinct Population Segments with different statuses in different places — the gray wolf spotlighted above is one species with six active or historical entries. This page is built around entities, the government's own unit of record, and says so wherever the two counts might be confused.
  • Reclassification history doesn't join cleanly. The reclassification report and the master species table describe the same population in different words in a small number of cases — a short label ("Western DPS") in one, a full range description in the other. This build resolves the difference two ways: a lookup through a third report that carries both forms, and a last-resort word-overlap match for the handful that still don't agree.
  • 27 listing entities in this dataset carry no listing date at all in FWS's own export, almost all older foreign listings with incomplete records. They're excluded from every date-based chart on this page, and shown as "date not recorded" in the explorer.
  • Critical habitat acreage is not additive. The figures in the habitat section are parsed from each designation's own recorded text and can overlap across amendments to the same designation.
  • The expenditure figures are self-reported, lagging, and parsed out of a PDF. Section 18 obliges FWS to collect what every federal agency and every state says it spent; nobody audits the returns, and an agency that attributes a large shared programme to one species moves the ranking on its own. FY2022 is the most recent report published — it appeared in May 2026. The reports exist only as PDFs, so the tables were extracted from the text layer; the parser refuses to write a year whose rows do not sum to the total the report prints for itself, and all four years reconcile exactly. Land acquisition and multi-species work the report cannot attribute to one entity are excluded by the report, not by this page.
  • “NOAA-led” is a union of two sources that disagree with each other. A spending row counts as NOAA's if NOAA's own species directory names the species, or if ECOS records NMFS as lead agency. Either source alone undercounts: ECOS misses several NOAA species under the names NOAA uses, and NOAA's directory is organised by species while the money is reported per listing entity. Between 89% and 96% of each year's dollars also join to a specific ECOS entity, but the ranked lists above are taken from the report before any joining, so the join's misses cannot move them.
  • The PBR comparison is Atlantic and Gulf only. Only the Atlantic volume of the 2024 stock assessment cycle carries a cross-stock summary table; the Pacific and Alaska volumes that year are revision volumes covering only the stocks revised in that cycle. So “stocks over PBR” here means Atlantic and Gulf stocks, not national ones. PBR is also undefined for a stock with no current abundance estimate, which is why only 69 of 116 stocks can be compared at all — the ones with no number are not therefore safe.
  • Federal Register counts are a keyword search, not a register of ESA actions. They count documents each agency filed whose text matches “Endangered and Threatened”. That catches the large majority of ESA rulemaking, because both agencies head their ESA documents that way, but it misses documents phrased differently and catches a few that merely mention the phrase. Per-species counts search the scientific name as a phrase, so a species the two agencies name differently is undercounted under one of them.
  • A global rank is not a verdict on a listing. NatureServe ranks a species across its entire range; the ESA lists distinct population segments and evolutionarily significant units within it. A G5 species with listed runs is not evidence of an unnecessary listing — it is the Act's population unit working exactly as designed. 52 of the 105 NOAA species carry no NatureServe rank at all, most of them Indo-Pacific corals.
  • This page tracks species with a status of Listed or Delisted only. Candidates, proposed listings, and species considered and rejected are real parts of the process but are out of scope for the main explorer; a handful are referenced in passing above.
